The use case that will be the focus of this thesis is Suppression of Enemy Air Defense (SEAD). SEAD is a mission type that aims to enable friendly air superiority and freedom of movement by disruption or destruction of enemy air defense. Enemy threats advance faster in technical improvements and require an answer through technological developments on friendly side as well. An increase in the complexity of how interwoven these systems are using robotics, virtual reality, etc., require new methods in implementing solutions too. (Speed & Stathopoulos, 2018)

_Note 9/11: Use of AI in embedded engineering, criteria required for embedded_

With a need to find new technologies to be used in military, it's important to know where this is already being research and implemented. One such development is with the integration of AI in software and systems. The integration of AI into engineering solutions in embedded systems has been done across various domains, including healthcare (Aziz _et al._, 2026), industrial IoT (Dini _et al._, 2024) and in electric and hybrid vehicles (Waheed and Abro, 2026). 
Here it will be analyzed for the domain of avionic systems, which is a subdomain of embedded systems, how AI planning can be used in the context of SEAD with a hierarchical task network (HTN) in a safety-critical system. HTNs are well suited in domains where a hierarchical representation is desirable (Georgievski and Aiello, 2015). The hierarchical model can also be seen in military structures and organization across strategy, operations and tactics (Nagele, 2023) which is a good match with HTN.
Firstly, the definition of what an HTN is will be introduced and then applied to an example of one open-source implementation that demonstrates a scenario applicable to SEAD. After this, the topic of redundancy engineering will be introduced and why it is required.

_Note 15/10/26: MBSE method of work, system definition_

The next step is to state *how* the system-level problem of a SPOF can be solved, which is done by introducing triple modular redundancy (TMR). The decision for TMR is rooted in the common use within avionic and other safety-critical domains, this applies to both hardware and software systems. Hence the architecture defines no restriction on a concept level of how TMR is realized in its technical implementation, both software and hardware may be viable depending on the specific system.

Using the model above, it is simpler to define what other components are necessary to use TMR as a part of the process to mitigate a possible failure. Input would come from other systems, e.g. sensors, sub-systems, etc., and needs to be received by the process. The process then passes forward its output to other sub-systems, e.g. actuators. One principle found in best practices can be applied here, by providing each component only with necessary information which it requires for the functions it is defined for.
