DFS is a deterministic search algorithm in good agreement with behavior per-node in a cluster of redundant nodes. Hence why heuristic and stochastic planners are being excluded from the scope as they can introduce non-deterministic behavior.

Moving on to network behavior and topology, the only relevant part is that a static cluster will be considered because a close-world state allows for full offline definition at time of design and development for any mechanisms which TMR and HTN requires. Dynamic management or ad-hoc peer-to-peer planning introduces unnecessary complexity, deviating from the main question of the thesis.

Connecting this to the topic of communication within the nodes is the possibility of errors in either transmission medium or the message itself even at successful delivery at the recipient. While message delivery is defined as guaranteed between sender and recipient, it is still possible that errors occur on a bit-level due to electromagnetic variance or hardware problems in electronic components, e.g. CPU, RAM, a bus system or other which aren't a fault of the actual transmission itself. The guarantee for message delivery is assumed based on the existing standards for avionic buses, e.g. MIL-STD-1553 **add source** which defines mechanisms for retransmission and bit-level correction like parity bits, Manchester II, etc.

After this, the next topic is on which types of redundancy will be analyzed. Active Spatial Replication is included as it's a foundational concept used widely in many safety-critical domains including but not limited to avionic, automotive, medical systems. Replication in the form of work splitting and parallelization is excluded as this is meant for speeding up processing time to optimize for processing time with available resources, not for ensuring resilience under typical failures during runtime.

Then looking at which types of redundancy can be found in the hardware level and at the software level, two specific concepts are excluded from the scope. N-version programming is a process topic combined with engineering which is outside of the scope of question. Same goes for design of the software itself which would be close to analyzing verifiable coding guidelines and methods adhering to standard specifications such as DO-178C and ISO 26262. This would divert the focus into process engineering spanning from requirements definition, testing to formal verification methods.

The last part currently excluded for concept development are advanced architectures which make use of TMR as one component of the redundancy model. Focus will instead be put on concepts from these architectures to a more basic model with cross-domain referencing as a method of reasoning.
